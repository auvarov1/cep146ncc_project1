Today we will discuss about Artificial Intelligence (AI) and separate common misconceptions and fear from the actual facts and problems we are facing today. 

Let's start with the panic most people are feeling about automation and unemployment.

It is important to remember history has, repeatedly, shown the market adapting and readjusting itself during big technological waves.

Instead of creating mass unemployment, these innovations create new products and jobs. The focus of work simply shifts.

The fear that artificial intelligence is about to upend Canadian labour markets is viewing the picture through a distorted lens.

What the data actually shows is that, yes, the adoption of AI by companies it is growing, but at a steady pace with no evidence of rapid acceleration. 

There was a growth of 13% over the last 2 years of companies adopting AI in Canada, and it is concentrated in finance and insurance, professional, scientific and technical services, and information and culture industries.

And almost 90% of companies that have already adopted AI have reported zero change in their staff. 

At least for these initial years of experimentation, AI has been used to complement human labour, not a complete replacement for it.

However, when we start talking about responsibility and accountability, this is where the actual danger awaits.

As AI becomes more autonomous and grow in different critical spheres of life traditional quality and control methods stop working.

When mistakes are made by a person or a company at a legal level, they have to justify their reasoning to a forum. 

That raises the issue of the, so-called culpability gap. We often look to find one person to be guilty, but when this mistakes comes from an autonomous algorithm its a lot harder to punish anyone.

How can anyone have accountability for something a machine has done? AI has no explicit moral standards, it is not living to be accountable and punished by its decisions, it makes no sense.

Some may say accountability for anything that happens on those cases lies to the developers, those who reviewed it, or the company that blindly trusted it. 

However, in reality, we have these problems with moral accountability. Developers almost never know how to explain the decision of complex models, and customers and authorities don't have understandable explanation.

The decision-making logic is hidden even from the creators themselves, creating a "Black-box".

Consider the Air Canada chatbot case, for instance,

Basically, the chatbot invented and promised a discount for a costomer and the company tried to put the AI at fault.

But guess what, that excuse was denied.

The tribunal ruled against the airline, stating clearly that a company is fully responsible for everything its system communicates.

It is impossible to solve these problems without new standards of transparency, rigorous system audits, and reliable state regulation.
